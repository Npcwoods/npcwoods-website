By Chris Woods, MSN, APRN, FNP-C
Board-certified nurse practitioner · Reviewed October 6, 2026

Last stop, you got the big picture. Now let's follow GLP-1 all the way from your fork to your brain, and you won't need a science degree.

The short version

  • You eat, and your gut rings a doorbell called GLP-1.
  • GLP-1 is a key that fits a lock on cells in your pancreas, stomach, and brain.
  • A bouncer named DPP-4 tosses your own GLP-1 out in about two minutes.
  • The medicine dodges the bouncer, so one shot lasts about a week.
Step 1 · The doorbell

You eat. Your gut rings the bell.

When food reaches your small gut, special cells in the gut wall called L-cells notice it. Yes, your gut can sort of "taste" food. Weird, but true.

The L-cells answer by sending out a tiny hormone messenger called GLP-1. Think of it as a doorbell that tells your whole body, "Food is here!"[1][2]

Why it mattersThe medicines copy this real signal from your own body. They don't invent a brand-new one.

Step 2 · Lock and key

GLP-1 is a key. Your cells have the lock.

The lock is called the GLP-1 receptor. It's a protein that snakes back and forth through the cell wall, like a lock built into a front door.[1]

When the key turns, a G protein inside the cell switches on a helper called cAMP. Think of cAMP as the cell's group text, because one ping tells the whole cell what to do.[2]

Pancreas: beta cells

They release more insulin, but mostly when your blood sugar is high.

Pancreas: alpha cells

They release less glucagon, the hormone that pushes blood sugar up.

Stomach

It empties more slowly. You'll see why that matters in Step 4.

Brain

Your hypothalamus and brainstem, the hunger and fullness centers, get the "I'm full" memo.

Why it mattersThe insulin push mostly happens when sugar is high, so low blood sugar is not common on these medicines alone. The risk goes up if you also take insulin or a sulfonylurea.[1]

Step 3 · The bouncer

Meet DPP-4, the bouncer.

Your body has a bouncer, an enzyme named DPP-4, and its whole job is to toss GLP-1 out fast. Your own GLP-1 lasts only about two minutes before it's gone.[1][3]

So scientists gave the medicine two clever upgrades.

  1. A swapped link. They changed one building block near the front of the chain, right where the bouncer grabs. Now he can't get a grip.
  2. A fatty tail. It hangs onto albumin, a big protein that floats in your blood. The medicine rides it like a bus, so your kidneys can't flush it out quickly.[3]

Why it mattersSemaglutide lasts about a week, and tirzepatide lasts about five days. That's why the shot is once a week instead of every meal.[5][6]

Step 4 · The yellow light

Your stomach hits a yellow light.

GLP-1 tells your stomach to empty more slowly. Think yellow light, not red light, because food still moves along. It just takes its time.[1]

Since food stays in your stomach longer, you tend to feel full longer. Your brain gets the "full" memo too, so it often gets easier to stop eating.[2]

Here's the catch. A slower stomach is also a big reason some people feel queasy at first, and that's our next stop.

Bonus · Two keys

Tirzepatide carries a second key.

Semaglutide fits one lock, the GLP-1 receptor. Tirzepatide fits two locks: the GLP-1 receptor and the GIP receptor.[4][6]

GIP is another gut doorbell, and it also helps your pancreas release insulin after you eat. So tirzepatide is like one key with two cuts.

Is two keys better for you? That depends on your health history, and it's something we sort out together.

Brand namesSemaglutide is in Ozempic® and Wegovy®. Tirzepatide is in Mounjaro® and Zepbound®.

Cheat sheet

Four pictures. That's it.

Doorbell

After you eat, L-cells in your gut send out GLP-1.

Lock and key

GLP-1 fits its receptor on your pancreas, stomach, and brain.

Bouncer

DPP-4 cuts your own GLP-1 fast, but the medicine dodges it.

Yellow light

Your stomach empties more slowly, so you feel full sooner.

Quick questions

You asked. I answered.

Where does my own GLP-1 come from?

L-cells in your gut make it after you eat, and it only lasts about two minutes.

Why does one shot last a week?

The medicine dodges the DPP-4 bouncer and rides on albumin in your blood, so it sticks around for days.

What is different about tirzepatide?

It fits two locks, GLP-1 and GIP. Think of one key with two cuts.

Can it drop my blood sugar too low?

On its own, not often, because it mostly works when your sugar is high. The risk goes up with insulin or a sulfonylurea, so tell me every medicine you take.
