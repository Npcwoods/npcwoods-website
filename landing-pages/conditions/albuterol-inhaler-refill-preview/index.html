Red flags — these need emergency care right now

If you or someone you’re with has any of these symptoms, do not wait. Do not text me. Call 911 or get to the nearest ER immediately.

  • Severe difficulty breathing — you can’t finish a sentence or catch your breath between words
  • Lips, fingernails, or face turning blue or gray
  • Ribs or neck muscles visibly pulling in with each breath (retractions)
  • Albuterol isn’t helping at all — you’ve used it and you’re still getting worse
  • Chest tightness so severe you feel like you can’t breathe in
  • Confusion, drowsiness, or difficulty staying awake during an attack
  • A child who stops crying or playing because they’re working too hard to breathe

Asthma attacks can be life-threatening. When in doubt, always go to the ER. You can text me later when you’re stable. Your safety is more important than a $59 visit.



⚠️ Warning signs — text me, but watch closely

Yellow flags — these deserve attention today

These situations aren’t 911 emergencies, but they mean your asthma isn’t under good control. Text me about these during your visit — this is exactly what the $59 consult covers.

  • Waking up at night because of coughing, wheezing, or chest tightness more than twice a month
  • Using your rescue inhaler more than twice a week (not counting exercise pre-treatment) — this is a sign you may need a controller medication
  • Going through inhalers faster than expected — a 200-puff canister shouldn’t be empty in a few weeks
  • Avoiding activities like exercise, yard work, or playing with your kids because you’re worried about triggering an attack
  • Needing your inhaler before everyday activities — walking upstairs, carrying groceries, or just keeping up with your normal routine
  • More than one asthma flare-up in the past year that needed oral steroids or an ER visit

If any of these sound familiar, a refill alone might not be enough. I can talk through whether a maintenance inhaler could help you need the rescue less. That conversation is part of the visit.


What albuterol actually does (plain English version)

Albuterol is a rescue bronchodilator. When your airways tighten up — from asthma triggers, exercise, cold air, allergens, or just because lungs are unpredictable sometimes — albuterol relaxes the smooth muscles around your bronchial tubes. Think of it like this: your airways are garden hoses that occasionally crimp shut. Albuterol un-kinks the hose so air can flow again.

It works fast, usually within minutes, and the effects typically last 4-6 hours. That’s why it’s called a “rescue” inhaler — it’s your emergency exit when breathing gets tight.

The catch? It treats the symptom (tight airways), not the underlying inflammation. If you’re reaching for it constantly, the fire’s still burning — you’re just blowing smoke out of the room without putting out the flames.

Using your rescue inhaler too often? Let’s talk about that.

Here’s a rule of thumb most people don’t know: if you’re using your albuterol rescue inhaler more than twice a week (not counting before exercise), your asthma may not be as well-controlled as it could be.

That doesn’t mean you’re doing anything wrong. It means a maintenance inhaler (also called a controller) might help. Controller medications work differently — they reduce the inflammation in your airways over time so flare-ups happen less often. Think of albuterol as a fire extinguisher and a controller as a sprinkler system. You want both, but you’d rather not need the extinguisher every day.

Common controllers include inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) and combination inhalers. During your $59 visit, I can talk through whether adding a controller makes sense for your situation. It’s part of the consult — no extra charge.

Questions I get asked a lot

Can you prescribe the brand-name inhaler I’ve been using?

I prescribe generic albuterol HFA (the metered-dose inhaler). It’s the same active medication. If you have a specific brand preference, let me know during the visit and I’ll see what I can do, but generic is what most pharmacies stock and it’s typically the most affordable option.

What if my pharmacy is out of stock?

I can send the prescription to a different pharmacy if needed. Just let me know during the visit or text me afterward — I can transfer it.

Do I need to send you my previous prescription?

It helps, but it’s not always required. I’ll ask about your diagnosis history, who prescribed it before, and how long you’ve been using albuterol. If I can verify your history, we can move forward.

How many refills will I get?

This depends on your individual situation. I’ll typically prescribe what’s clinically appropriate — which is usually enough to get you through until your next check-in or primary care visit.
