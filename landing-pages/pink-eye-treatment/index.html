A fast, thoughtful starting point

One symptom. Different possible causes.

Pink eye is a common name for conjunctivitis. Symptoms can overlap, so the goal is not to guess—it is to get the right next step.

Often watery

Viral patterns

Watery discharge and a cold-like illness can occur together. Many cases improve with supportive care rather than prescription treatment.

Often thicker discharge

Bacterial patterns

Thicker discharge that makes the eyelids stick together can be one clue a clinician considers alongside your history.

Often very itchy

Allergy patterns

Itching in both eyes with other allergy symptoms can point in a different direction and is not contagious.

Some eye symptoms need in-person care now.

Eye pain, light sensitivity, blurred vision that does not improve after wiping discharge away, intense redness, an eye injury, worsening symptoms, or a contact-lens concern are reasons to seek prompt in-person eye care. Newborns with pink-eye symptoms need care right away. Text-based telehealth is not for emergencies. If you have chest pain, trouble breathing, or other emergency symptoms, call 911.

Common questions

Before you text

Can I wear contact lenses with pink eye?

Stop wearing contact lenses while you have symptoms. Contact-lens wearers with red-eye symptoms need prompt in-person eye care because the concern can be more serious than routine conjunctivitis.

Is pink eye contagious?

Viral and bacterial conjunctivitis can spread easily. Allergic and irritant-related eye symptoms are not contagious. Wash hands often, avoid rubbing your eyes, and do not share towels, makeup, or eye products.

Will I need prescription drops?

Not every case needs them. Viral and allergic conjunctivitis do not respond to antibiotic drops. A clinician will assess what you share and help determine the right next step.

What can I do while I wait?

Cold compresses and artificial tears may help with discomfort. Do not use someone else’s drops or share eye products.
