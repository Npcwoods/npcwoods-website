What GLP-1 actually is

GLP-1 stands for glucagon-like peptide-1. Your small intestine makes it after you eat. Think of it as your gut's short text message to the rest of you: food is here, handle it well.

That message hits several places at once. The pancreas can release insulin when sugar is high. It can dial down glucagon (the "raise sugar" hormone). The stomach empties a bit slower. The brain feels fuller sooner.

Your natural GLP-1 vanishes fast — about two minutes — because an enzyme called DPP-4 chops it up. The medicines are built to last much longer, often about a week per dose for the weekly shots.

Still with me? Good. I use four simple pictures for the rest of this series. Once you have them, everything else gets easier.

Four pictures that make it click

1. The doorbell

Your gut rings the brain's doorbell after you eat: "Dinner's here." That signal helps with appetite and fullness.

2. Lock & key

The receptor is the lock. The medicine is the key that fits — and stays. That's how a shot can keep working for days.

3. The bouncer (DPP-4)

DPP-4 is the bouncer that kicks natural GLP-1 out in about two minutes. Medicines are built so the bouncer can't toss them as fast.

4. The yellow light

Slow stomach emptying is like a yellow traffic light for food leaving. Helpful for fullness — and a big reason tummy side effects show up if you overdo greasy or huge meals.

Why this matters: If you understand the doorbell, the lock-and-key, the bouncer, and the yellow traffic light, the ads and the side effects suddenly make sense. That's the point of this series.
