By Chris Woods, MSN, APRN, FNP-C
Board-certified nurse practitioner · Reviewed October 6, 2026

Last stop, your stomach got a yellow light. That one change explains most side effects, so here's the honest list and what to do about it.

The short version

  • Most side effects live in your tummy: queasy, loose, or backed up.
  • They show up most at the start and after a dose step, then often ease.
  • Small meals, slow bites, less grease, and plenty of water help a lot.
  • A few rare signs mean you should act now, so learn them below.
Why it happens

Why your tummy talks back.

Remember the yellow light? Food sits in your stomach longer than it used to. Add a big meal on top, and it's like rush hour at a yellow light. Traffic backs up, and hello, queasy.[1][5]

Your gut also needs time to get used to a brand-new signal, which is why I start low and go slow.[1][2]

Why it mattersMost side effects live in your tummy. They're most common at the start and after a dose step, and for many people they ease with time.[1][2]

The common list

What lots of people feel.

Here are the ones I hear about most. You may get none of them, or you may get a few.[1][2]

Most common

Feeling sick to your stomach, loose stools, getting backed up, and throwing up.

Also common

Belly pain, heartburn or burping, feeling tired, headaches, and feeling dizzy.

At the shot spot

Some redness or itching, which often fades in a day or two.

What helps

Small moves. Big relief.

These tips come straight from how the medicine works. If your stomach is slower, give it less work to do.[1][2]

Eat small

Use a smaller plate, and stop when you're almost full instead of stuffed.

Slow down

Put your fork down between bites, because your brain needs a minute to catch up.

Go easy on grease

Fried and fatty foods sit the longest, so they're a top queasy trigger.

Sip all day

Water helps your gut and protects your kidneys, and small sips count.

Fiber plus water

If you're backed up, add veggies, fruit, and beans, then drink a little more.

Stay upright

Wait a while after eating before you lie down.

Bland when queasy

Crackers, toast, rice, and broth are boring, but boring is your friend.

Text me early

Still feeling rough before a dose step? Tell me, because we can slow down.

Rare, but real

Know these red flags.

These are not common, but if one shows up, act fast. For most of them, that means the ER or 911.[1][2][3][4]

Bad belly pain

Strong pain that won't quit and may spread to your back, with or without throwing up. This can be your pancreas.

Upper right belly pain

Pain under your right ribs, a fever, or yellow skin or eyes. This can be your gallbladder.

Can't keep fluids down

Very little pee, dark pee, or feeling dizzy when you stand. Losing too much fluid can hurt your kidneys.

Allergic reaction

A swollen face, lips, tongue, or throat, trouble breathing, or a fast, bad rash.

Low blood sugar

Feeling shaky, sweaty, or confused, or a racing heart. Eat or drink some sugar right away. The risk is higher with insulin or a sulfonylurea.

Dark thoughts

New mood changes or thoughts of hurting yourself. Call or text 988 any time, day or night.

Surgery coming up?Before any surgery or sedation, tell the care team you take a GLP-1. Food can still be sitting in a slow stomach, which raises the risk of breathing it into your lungs.[3][4]

Two label rules

The fine print, made plain.

The boxed warning

In studies with rats and mice, these medicines caused thyroid C-cell tumors. We don't know if this happens in people.[3][4]

Don't take one if you or anyone in your family has had medullary thyroid cancer (MTC), or if you have a condition called MEN 2. Tell me about a lump in your neck, trouble swallowing, or a hoarse voice that won't go away.

Pregnancy and birth control

Planning a baby? With Wegovy®, stop at least 2 months before you start trying.[3] Zepbound® can make birth control pills work less well, so add a backup like condoms for 4 weeks after you start. Then do it again for 4 weeks after each dose step.[4]

If you get pregnant, text me right away.

Who to call

Text me, or go to the ER?

Here's my simple rule. If it's annoying, text me. If it's scary, get help right now.

Text me

  • Queasy that won't ease
  • Backed up for more than a few days
  • Can't eat much at all
  • Missed a dose
  • Planning a baby or surgery
  • New medicine from someone else

ER or 911

  • Bad belly pain that won't quit
  • Can't keep any fluids down
  • Swelling of face or throat, or hard to breathe
  • Fainting or chest pain
  • Thoughts of hurting yourself (or call or text 988)
Cheat sheet

The whole page in four cards.

Why

A slow stomach plus a big meal equals a traffic jam.

When

Most often at the start and after each dose step.

What helps

Eat small, eat slow, skip the grease, and sip water.

Act now

Bad belly pain, no fluids staying down, or allergy signs mean the ER.

Quick questions

You asked. I answered.

Will feeling sick last forever?

For many people, it eases after the first few weeks. It can come back a little after a dose step, so tell me if it doesn't ease.

Can I take something for nausea?

Text me first, and I'll check your other medicines to see what makes sense for you.

Why tell my surgery team?

Your stomach empties more slowly, so food may still be there during sedation. That raises the risk of breathing it into your lungs.

Is getting backed up normal?

It's common. Water, fiber, and moving your body all help. Text me if it lasts more than a few days or you have bad pain.
