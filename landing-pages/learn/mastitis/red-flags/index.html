Most mastitis is miserable and still manageable. A few cases spread, form an abscess, or tip into a body-wide infection called sepsis. I want this list on your fridge. If you meet it, you do not text me first. You go.

The short version

  • Fever of 103°F or higher: go in now.
  • Red streaks spreading fast, shaking chills, or a racing heart: go now.
  • Feeling faint, confused, or very sick: call 911.
  • Pus or a soft, growing lump needs care today.
Red flag · call 911

Go to the ER or call 911 now

Go now. Do not text first.

A fever of 103°F or higher. Red streaks spreading fast toward the armpit or chest. Shaking chills that will not stop. A racing heart or fast breathing. Feeling faint, dizzy, or confused. Feeling very sick all over. Those are not text-visit problems.

Part 1

Get in-person care today

  1. Pus draining from the breast or nipple.
  2. A soft, squishy, or growing lump: possible abscess. It needs an ultrasound.
  3. Redness spreading even with care.
  4. Both breasts red and hot at the same time.
  5. Not better after 48 hours of treatment.
  6. A weak immune system, diabetes, or a recent breast surgery.
Part 2

Why spreading matters

Infection that spreads into skin and tissue can move fast. Sepsis is the body overreacting to an infection. Early signs are fever or feeling cold, a fast heartbeat, fast breathing, confusion, and extreme pain or discomfort. Sepsis is an emergency. Do not wait for morning.

Part 3

What a text visit is not for

I will not talk you out of the ER. If your story sounds like any of the above, I send you in. You do not pay the $59 when I tell you text care is not safe.

Quick questions

You asked. I answered.

My fever is 101 and one breast is red. ER or text?

If you feel okay otherwise, can eat and drink, and the redness is not spreading fast, that often fits a same-day text visit. If you feel very sick, faint, or confused, go in now.

How do I know if it is an abscess?

A lump that stays or grows, feels soft or fluid-filled, or drains pus. It needs an ultrasound, and often drainage with a needle.

Should I stop breastfeeding if I go to the ER?

Usually no. Tell the ER team you are breastfeeding so they can choose care that fits.
